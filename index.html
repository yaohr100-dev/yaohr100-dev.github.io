NBA Analysis: Estimated Wins

Using five seasons of play-by-play data and EPM (Estimated Plus-Minus), advanced analytics can answer one question: how many wins does a single player really add — stripped of teammate luck, opponent strength, and random noise.

1. How to Scrape the Data

📡 DATA SOURCES

  • NBA.com Stats API
    Official play-by-play, box scores, and tracking data. Hidden JSON endpoints power their public pages — browser DevTools reveal them.
  • Basketball-Reference
    Clean historical tables — PER, BPM, VORP, on/off splits. The gold standard for cross-referencing.
  • Dunks & Threes
    Home of EPM, Estimated Skills, and Estimated Wins data. Their methodology page is essential reading.
  • pbpstats.com
    Processed play-by-play — lineup data, possession-level stats. Saves hours of raw cleaning.

🛠 SCRAPING TOOLS

  • Python + requests / httpx
    Send HTTP requests to API endpoints, handle rate limits and retry logic.
  • BeautifulSoup / lxml
    Parse HTML tables from Basketball-Reference when JSON APIs aren't available.
  • Selenium / Playwright
    For JavaScript-rendered pages — render, wait, extract, export to CSV.
  • nba_api (Python library)
    Community-built wrapper around NBA.com endpoints. One-liner to pull play-by-play or shot-chart data.

2. How to Clean the Data

🔧 MANUAL APPROACH (Understand First)

  • Excel / Google Sheets
    Start small — paste a single season, sort by MP (minutes played), remove garbage-time rows. You build intuition for the data's shape.
  • Hand-labeling
    Verify player name mappings (e.g. "James Harden" vs "J. Harden" across sources). Catch encoding issues early before they corrupt the model.
  • VLOOKUP / INDEX-MATCH
    Join multiple tables — EPM data × box scores × lineup data — in a spreadsheet to validate that joins work before coding them.

🤖 AUTOMATED PIPELINE (Scale Up)

  • Pandas (Python)
    .merge() for joining sources, .dropna() with thresholds, .groupby('player_id') to aggregate per-player metrics.
  • DuckDB / SQL
    When datasets exceed RAM — query 5 seasons of play-by-play directly with SQL, zero loading wait.
  • OpenRefine
    Detect and merge messy player-name variants (accents, suffixes like Jr./III) with clustering algorithms.

3. Analytical Capability: Modeling & Algorithms

SPM — STATISTICS PLUS-MINUS

Purely raw plus-minus suffers from wild year-to-year variance — short-term hot streaks or weak bench-mates create noise that drowns the signal. By adding box-score statistics (points, rebounds, assists, etc.) into the regression, SPM becomes far more stable and predictive of future performance. It turns the question from "what happened when this player was on the court?" into "what does this player's statistical footprint say about their impact?"


Key insight — weighted recency: the most recent season carries the most weight (×0.50), the second-to-last less (×0.33), and three years ago is largely faded out (×0.17). Rookies get a default SPM of −2.16, acknowledging that most first-year players are net negatives. This becomes the foundation: a player's statistical projection.


The Celtics test case: using SPM alone, the 2007-08 Boston Celtics — newly assembled with Pierce, Garnett, and Allen — were projected to win 67.5 games. They actually won 66. The model knew they'd be great before they'd played a single minute together. Across all teams the average prediction error was just 4.5 wins, beating the Pythagorean expectation formula. (Source: Basketball-Reference SPM article)


Technically, this is a linear regression where the dependent variable is a possession-level efficiency differential, and the features are each player's individual box-score contributions — rebounding rate, assist rate, turnover rate, usage, and shot-efficiency metrics. The model separates a player's contribution from the noise of who they share the floor with by including all ten players on the court as simultaneous variables. This gives you a per-possession impact estimate that can be scaled to "points per 100 possessions" — the currency of basketball analytics.

APM — ADJUSTED PLUS-MINUS

Raw plus-minus is unfair — if you consistently play alongside superstars, your numbers look better than you are. If you're always tasked with guarding the opponent's best player, your numbers look worse than you deserve. APM was the first serious attempt to solve this by building a regression model where every player on the court is a variable, and the dependent variable is the point differential during each stint.


Each 10-man lineup segment becomes one row in the regression: ten player coefficients plus a home-court advantage term. The model solves for each player's partial effect — "holding the other nine players constant, how many points per 100 possessions does this player add?" This is the same intellectual framework as a controlled experiment, except the experiment is the NBA season itself.


The fatal flaw: overfitting. When a rookie plays five good games, APM can assign them a superstar-level rating because the model has no way to distinguish "real skill" from "small-sample luck." It treats every observation as equally meaningful. A player shooting 67% over a handful of games looks like the greatest shooter in history to a naive APM model. This is where regularization enters — and where the methodology gets genuinely elegant.


Technically, the APM model is an ordinary least squares regression with indicator variables for each player. The design matrix is sparse — most players don't appear in most stints — which makes the coefficient estimates unstable. Small perturbations in the data produce large swings in individual ratings. Understanding this instability is critical: it tells you how much you don't know, which is just as important as the point estimate itself.

RAPM — REGULARIZED ADJUSTED PLUS-MINUS

RAPM applies ridge regression (L2 regularization) to the APM framework — and this single addition transforms the model. Instead of treating every player's rating as a free parameter, ridge regression imposes a penalty: extreme values are pulled back toward zero unless the data is overwhelmingly convincing. The penalty term λ (lambda) controls how strong this "shrinkage" is — think of it as a knob between "trust every observation equally" (λ→0, pure APM) and "assume everyone is average" (λ→∞).


The key insight from Sill's original RAPM paper is that we should start with a prior belief: most NBA players fall within a reasonable range, roughly [−6, +6] points per 100 possessions. When the model sees a small-sample explosion — say a bench player going +15 over five games — ridge regression says "most of that is noise, let's regress it toward the mean." The result: ratings that are less extreme in a single season but far more predictive of the next season.


Cross-validation is used to choose λ — split the data by time periods, fit the model on one portion, test on the held-out future games. The λ that minimizes out-of-sample prediction error is selected. This is the same technique used throughout machine learning for hyperparameter tuning, applied to basketball. The elegance is that λ is not chosen by gut feel — it emerges from the data itself.


Multi-year RAPM: an extension where multiple seasons are stacked together, with older seasons receiving less weight (exponential decay). This dramatically improves stability — instead of 1,000 possessions for a single-season estimate, you might have 5,000+ possessions across three seasons, giving the regression far more data to work with. The cost is that it smooths over genuine career arcs; players who have rapidly improved or declined are not captured instantly. This tension between stability and responsiveness is the central design challenge of all player-evaluation metrics.

EPM — ESTIMATED PLUS-MINUS (SPM + RAPM)

EPM is the synthesis: use SPM as the prior, then let RAPM adjust it with on/off-court evidence. This is a Bayesian framework — start with what the box score tells us (the prior), then update that belief with how the team actually performs when the player is on the floor (the likelihood). The result is the most robust single-number impact metric in public basketball analytics.


Concretely: SPM provides a stable baseline that doesn't overreact to small samples, because box-score stats stabilize quickly. RAPM provides the "did the team actually outscore opponents?" signal that captures everything the box score misses — screen-setting, defensive rotations, off-ball gravity, communication. EPM keeps what SPM gets right (stability, forward-predictability) and adds what RAPM uniquely captures (the unmeasured, the intangible).


From EPM, Estimated Wins are computed by aggregating each player's EPM across their minutes played, converting point differential to a win probability via the Pythagorean formula, and scaling to an 82-game season. A player with +5.0 EPM who plays 2,500 minutes contributes roughly 8–10 wins above a replacement-level player. This is the number that directly answers: "how many wins is this player worth?"

ESTIMATED SKILLS & DARKO (Kalman Filter)

If a player shoots 60% from three over ten games, is that real improvement or a hot streak? Estimated Skills answers this by applying a Kalman filter — the same algorithm used in rocket navigation — to every skill dimension (three-point shooting, finishing, playmaking, etc.). The filter maintains a running estimate of "true talent" that updates with each new game, but weighted by how much new information the game actually provides. A single game moves the estimate a tiny amount; a full season of sustained improvement moves it substantially.


DARKO (Daily Adjusted and Regressed Kalman Optimized projections) extends this with exponential decay weighting — games from last week matter more than games from last year — and explicitly models age curves, rest-advantage effects, opponent strength, and home/road splits. It predicts forward, answering "what will this player do tomorrow?" rather than "what did they do last season?"


The critical technical advantage: Kalman filters naturally handle the stability-responsiveness tradeoff. When observations are noisy (small sample), the filter trusts the prior more. When data accumulates, the estimate converges toward the true value. This is fundamentally superior to a simple moving average, which treats game 1 and game 82 as equally informative — they are not.

Cosmic Watch: Muon Detection & Atmospheric Analysis

Research Project on Data Analysis and Machine Learning · Jul–Oct 2024 · Supervised by MIT Professor Gunther Roland

1. Motivation: Why This Matters

Cosmic muons are elementary particles produced when high-energy cosmic rays collide with Earth's upper atmosphere. They rain down constantly — roughly one per square centimeter per minute at sea level. Because they penetrate matter deeply, detecting them reveals information about both the cosmos and our atmosphere.

The core question: atmospheric pressure acts like a shield — higher pressure means more air for muons to travel through, increasing the chance they decay before reaching our detector. By quantifying this relationship, we can correct for weather effects and sharpen our ability to detect rare cosmic events like high-energy particle bursts.

2. What I Did: Data Visualization & Analysis

TASK 1 — ATMOSPHERIC PRESSURE VS. TIME-STAMP

Used Python (Matplotlib + Pandas) to generate atmospheric-pressure-over-time plots for each data group. Each group contained tens of thousands of timestamped pressure readings from the Cosmic Watch detector.


After generating the images, I manually classified each plot — identifying patterns like sensor drift, diurnal pressure cycles, and anomalous spikes. This manual step was essential: it built an intuition for what "normal" detector behavior looks like, making it far easier to spot genuinely interesting physics events later.

TASK 2 — DIURNAL PRESSURE VISUALIZATION

Built a 24-hour heatmap visualization in Python showing how atmospheric pressure varies across each hour of the day, across multiple weeks of data. The goal: identify whether daily pressure cycles — warmer air expanding during the day, cooler denser air at night — systematically affected muon counts.


This revealed a clear diurnal pattern: muon detection rates were consistently lower during midday hours when atmospheric expansion reduced air density, and higher during the cooler nighttime hours. Quantifying this cycle meant we could subtract it out and isolate pressure-driven effects from temperature-driven ones.

TASK 3 — COINCIDENT EVENT RATE CALCULATION

Cosmic Watch uses two scintillator panels — a true muon event is recorded only when both panels trigger within a narrow time window. In Excel, I calculated the coincident event rate: counting how many paired triggers occurred per unit time, and computing the false-coincidence background rate from uncorrelated single-panel hits.


This directly fed into the time-difference calibration — by analyzing the distribution of time differences between the two panels, I identified a systematic offset that, once corrected, improved our sensitivity to rare coincidence events.

TASK 4 — TEAM SYNTHESIS & FINAL FIGURES

Consolidated all key visualizations generated by the team — pressure trends, diurnal heatmaps, coincidence timing distributions, and muon-rate vs. pressure scatter plots — into a unified presentation. I ensured each figure was properly labeled, consistently styled, and accompanied by explanatory captions so the entire analysis pipeline was reproducible and clear to any viewer.

3. Results & Key Findings

📉
Pressure vs. Muon Rate (Anti-Correlation)

As atmospheric pressure increases, muon detection rate decreases. Higher pressure = denser air = more muons decay before reaching ground level. This confirms the theoretical expectation and allows us to correct for weather artifacts in real-time cosmic event monitoring.

⏱️
Improved Timing Calibration

The refined time-difference calibration eliminates a systematic offset in coincidence detection. This directly enhances sensitivity to rare cosmic phenomena — including high-energy particle bursts and potentially exotics beyond the Standard Model.

4. Challenges & What I Learned

⚡ The noise problem. Raw detector data is noisy — electrical interference, temperature drift, and random coincidences all masquerade as real muon events. Distinguishing signal from noise required iterative filtering: first a simple threshold cut, then a statistical significance test on coincidence timing. This taught me that data cleaning is not a one-pass operation — it's a loop of hypothesis, filter, visualize, and refine.


🔄 Manual classification as a forcing function. Having to manually classify hundreds of pressure-time plots felt tedious at first, but it forced me to truly understand the data's texture — sensor drift patterns, the shape of diurnal cycles, the subtle difference between a real pressure anomaly and a loose cable. This deep familiarity later made automated classification possible because I knew exactly what features to look for.


🔗 Connecting to NBA Analysis. This experience crystallized a connection to my NBA work: both projects are fundamentally about separating signal from noise. In Cosmic Watch, atmospheric pressure is the confounder — just like teammate quality confounds raw plus-minus in basketball. The ridge regression that stabilizes RAPM is conceptually identical to the regularization we apply to time-difference distributions. Different domains, same underlying logic: control for what you can measure, regularize against what you cannot.
