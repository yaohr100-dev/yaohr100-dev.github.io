Deep DivesExplore the Details

Using five seasons of play-by-play data and EPM (Estimated Plus-Minus), you can estimate how many wins a single player adds — after accounting for teammates, opponents, and randomness.

1. Where the Data Comes From

Data sources:

  • NBA.com Stats API — official play-by-play, box scores, tracking data
  • Basketball-Reference — clean historical tables, PER, BPM, VORP
  • Dunks & Threes — EPM, Estimated Skills, and methodology
  • pbpstats.com — processed play-by-play, lineup data

Scraping tools: Python + requests / httpx · BeautifulSoup / lxml · Selenium / Playwright · nba_api library

2. Cleaning the Data

Manual (to get a feel for it): Excel / Google Sheets — paste a season, sort by minutes played, drop garbage-time rows. Hand-check player name mappings across sources. Use VLOOKUP to test joins before writing code.

Automated (for scale): Pandas (.merge(), .dropna(), .groupby()) · DuckDB / SQL when the data outgrows RAM · OpenRefine for fuzzy name matching.

3. The Models

SPM — Statistics Plus-Minus

Raw plus-minus jumps around too much year to year. Adding box-score stats (points, rebounds, assists, etc.) into a regression makes it more stable. The most recent season gets weight 0.50, the year before 0.33, three years ago 0.17. Rookies start at −2.16.

Example: the 2007-08 Celtics were projected at 67.5 wins by SPM before they'd played a game together. They won 66. The average error across all teams was 4.5 wins — better than the Pythagorean formula. (Source)

APM — Adjusted Plus-Minus

A regression where each 10-man lineup stint is a row, and each player is a variable. It answers: "holding the other nine guys constant, how many points per 100 possessions does this player add?" The problem is overfitting — a rookie who plays five good games can get a superstar rating because the model can't tell luck from skill.

RAPM — Regularized Adjusted Plus-Minus

Same idea, but with ridge regression (L2 penalty). Extreme values get pulled back toward zero unless the data is overwhelming. Cross-validation picks λ, the penalty strength. Stack multiple seasons with older games weighted less, and you get 5,000+ possessions per player instead of 1,000. (Sill's original paper)

EPM — Estimated Plus-Minus

EPM combines both approaches: SPM gives a stable baseline (what the box score says), and RAPM adjusts it with what actually happened when the player was on the floor. It's a Bayesian setup — start with a prior, update with evidence. To turn EPM into Estimated Wins: add up each player's EPM × minutes, convert the point differential to a win probability (Pythagorean formula), and scale to 82 games. A +5.0 EPM player logging 2,500 minutes is worth roughly 8–10 wins above replacement.

Estimated Skills & DARKO (Kalman Filter)

When a guy shoots 60% from three over 10 games, is he actually better or just on a heater? Estimated Skills runs a Kalman filter — same math as rocket guidance — on each skill (shooting, finishing, playmaking, etc.). The filter keeps a running "true talent" estimate that moves a little with each game but doesn't overreact. DARKO layers in exponential decay (recent games count more), age curves, rest effects, and opponent strength. It looks forward, not backward.

Research project supervised by MIT Professor Gunther Roland · Jul–Oct 2024. Read the paper.

1. Why This Matters

Cosmic muons are particles created when high-energy cosmic rays smash into the upper atmosphere. They rain down constantly — about one per square centimeter per minute at sea level. Atmospheric pressure works like a shield: more pressure means denser air, which means more muons decay before they reach the ground. If you can measure this relationship, you can subtract out the weather and get better at catching rare cosmic events.

2. What I Actually Did

Task 1 — Pressure vs. time plots. Used Matplotlib and Pandas to generate pressure-over-time charts for each data group (tens of thousands of timestamped readings per group). Then I manually sorted the plots — looking for sensor drift, day-night cycles, and anything that didn't belong. Doing it by hand was slow, but it taught me what "normal" looks like, which made it much easier to spot real anomalies later.

Task 2 — 24-hour heatmap. Built a heatmap in Python showing how pressure changes hour by hour across several weeks. A clear pattern showed up: muon counts were lower around midday (warmer, less dense air) and higher at night (cooler, denser air). Once we could quantify that cycle, we could remove it and focus on the pressure effects we actually cared about.

Task 3 — Coincidence rates. The detector uses two scintillator panels. A real muon event is when both panels fire within a tight time window. In Excel, I calculated the coincidence rate and the false-coincidence background. This fed directly into the timing calibration — analyzing the distribution of time gaps between the two panels turned up a systematic offset that, once fixed, made the detector more sensitive to rare events.

Task 4 — Pulling it together. I gathered all the team's visualizations — pressure trends, heatmaps, timing distributions, muon-rate vs. pressure scatter plots — into one presentation, with consistent labels and captions so anyone could follow the logic.

3. What We Found

Pressure vs. Muon Rate (Anti-Correlation)

Higher pressure → fewer muons detected. Matches what theory predicts: denser air means more muons decay before reaching the ground. This lets us correct for weather when monitoring for real cosmic events.

Improved Timing Calibration

Fixing a systematic offset in the coincidence timing made the detector more sensitive to genuine rare events — high-energy particle bursts and potentially stuff beyond the Standard Model.

4. What I Learned

Noise is the real problem. Raw detector data has electrical interference, temperature drift, and random coincidences all pretending to be muons. Pulling signal out of that took rounds of threshold cuts, statistical tests, and visual checks. Data cleaning isn't a one-and-done thing — it's a loop.

Manual work isn't wasted time. Sorting through hundreds of plots by hand felt tedious, but it forced me to learn how the data actually behaves — drift patterns, the shape of a diurnal cycle, the difference between a real pressure spike and a loose cable. That intuition made the automated stuff possible.

Same math, different problem. The ridge regression that stabilizes RAPM? Same idea as the regularization we applied to the timing distributions. In one case the confounder is teammate quality, in the other it's atmospheric pressure. Both problems boil down to: control what you can measure, regularize what you can't.
